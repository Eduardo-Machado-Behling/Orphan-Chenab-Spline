1. Start with the fitted knots

The Orphan–Chenab stream is a long trail of stars, likely stripped from a small galaxy. Astronomers measured its position, distance and motion to investigate the gravity of the Milky Way and the Large Magellanic Cloud.

Here we follow one quantity: , the motion along our line of sight. The coordinate tells us where we are along the trail. Velocity is expressed in the , correcting for the Sun’s motion.

The researchers fitted values at selected positions called . Those released values are our inputs. We reconstruct the curve between them; we are not fitting the original stars again.

2. Join the pieces smoothly

Straight lines pass through every knot, but their slopes usually jump at the joins. A spline uses a small polynomial on each interval. For a cubic spline, each piece has four coefficients:

Si(x) = a + bt + ct² + dt³
t = x − ni

We choose the pieces together so that their values, first derivatives and agree at each interior knot. That gives a curve with no breaks, no sudden slope changes and no jumps in its second derivative.

supplies the two remaining conditions: S″ = 0 at the first and last knots. This does not mean zero slope or a flat endpoint. It is a boundary choice, not a claim about the stream outside the data.

3. Solve for how the curve bends

Instead of solving for every polynomial coefficient at once, solve for zi = S″(ni), the second derivative at each knot. Think of it as how quickly the slope changes.

Let hi = ni+1 − ni be an interval’s width, and bi = (vi+1 − vi)/hi its straight-line slope. Each interior knot contributes one equation:

hi−1zi−1 + 2(hi−1 + hi)zi + hizi+1 = 6(bi − bi−1)

Only the previous, current and next z appear in a row. The matrix therefore has just three populated diagonals: it is .

The exploits that structure. A forward sweep eliminates the lower diagonal; back substitution recovers the unknowns. The solve takes linear time in the number of knots. The endpoint values stay fixed at zero.

Work through a small system by hand

Use the sandbox’s initial values: positions [0, 1, 2, 3, 4] and values [0, 1, 0, 1, 0]. Every width is 1. The straight-line slopes are [1, −1, 1, −1], so their successive changes give the right-hand side [−12, 12, −12]. With z₀ = z₄ = 0, only three unknowns remain:

⎡ 4  1  0 ⎤ ⎡ z₁ ⎤   ⎡ −12 ⎤
⎢ 1  4  1 ⎥ ⎢ z₂ ⎥ = ⎢  12 ⎥
⎣ 0  1  4 ⎦ ⎣ z₃ ⎦   ⎣ −12 ⎦
  1. Eliminate from left to right. Subtract ¼ of row 1 from row 2. Its equation becomes (15/4)z₂ + z₃ = 15. Subtract 4/15 of this new row 2 from row 3, leaving (56/15)z₃ = −16.
  2. Substitute from right to left. First z₃ = −30/7. Put that into row 2 to get z₂ = 36/7, then into row 1 to get z₁ = −30/7.

That is elimination without a dense matrix: each step only needs the neighboring row. Go to the sandbox and use its reset button to compare the displayed second derivatives with [0, −4.286, 5.143, −4.286, 0]. These are illustrative values, not stream measurements.

Connect the equation to the solver code

For an interior row k, c and d store the modified upper-diagonal and right-hand-side entries. The reduced diagonal is m.

const m = 2 * (h[k-1] + h[k])
        - h[k-1] * c[k-1];
c[k] = h[k] / m;
d[k] = (6 * (b[k] - b[k-1])
        - h[k-1] * d[k-1]) / m;

// Back substitution, from right to left:
z[k] = d[k] - c[k] * z[k+1];

Read the complete implementation. The original Stan method uses a dense solve; this page’s independent port solves the same system with Thomas elimination.

4. Evaluate the selected piece

Once we have the second derivatives, the cubic on any interval is determined. Find the interval containing x, measure t = x − ni from its left knot, then evaluate its polynomial.

The coefficients below are calculated from the published values and the solved second derivatives. They are another way to write the moment-form expression used in evaluate().

Where do the four coefficients come from?

a = vi
b = (vi+1 − vi)/h − h(2zi + zi+1)/6
c = zi/2
d = (zi+1 − zi)/(6h)

Here h is the current interval width. These local coefficients a, b, c, d are distinct from the solver’s temporary arrays. Integrating the linearly varying second derivative twice and matching the two knot values gives this expression.

The implementation uses half-open intervals: ni ≤ x < ni+1. It rejects the final knot itself and never extrapolates. The mathematical spline still has a defined value at that endpoint.

How a galaxy becomes a trail of stars

The Milky Way’s gravity pulls differently across a small galaxy. Stars escape from both sides and gradually spread along its path.

Illustration of . The Orphan–Chenab parent’s location is still unknown. The Large Magellanic Cloud is a companion galaxy whose gravity disturbs the trail.

How the measurements weigh two galaxies

Gravity bends the stream and changes its motion. The researchers simulated streams under different galaxy masses, then compared the results with the measurements.

  1. Measure the debris

    Map its sky position, distance and motion, including velocities across our view.

  2. Simulate its history

    Release stars from a small galaxy moving under the gravity of the Milky Way and its companion. Both galaxies move in response to each other.

  3. Compare with the data

    Find which masses best reproduce the stream’s positions and velocities, accounting for uncertainty.

After correcting for the Sun’s motion, the measured motions across the sky show southern stars moving across the trail toward the companion. This is one sign of the companion’s gravitational influence.

Mass inside a measured radius

Each result counts the mass inside a sphere centred on the named galaxy. “Sun masses” expresses that mass in units of our Sun’s mass.

The circles show cross-sections through the mass apertures on the same scale. The galaxy symbols are illustrative. Values are rounded as in the paper’s abstract, for its preferred model with a flattened Milky Way dark matter halo.

What can the stream tell us next?

Why does the trail spread out so much? Future comparisons could test how the parent galaxy lost its stars and how the gravity of the Milky Way and its companion shaped the debris.

These are open questions. The measured width and depth provide more information to test future models. Paper, section 6.1 and figure 19.

Reference: the full moment-form equation

With knots ni, values vi and spacings hi = ni+1 − ni, the interior second derivatives solve a tridiagonal system hi−1zi−1 + 2(hi−1+hi)zi + hizi+1 = 6(bi − bi−1), where bi = (vi+1−vi)/hi. The end values are fixed at z = 0. On [ni, ni+1] the curve is S(x) = zi+1(x−ni)³/6h + zi(ni+1−x)³/6h + (vi+1/h − hzi+1/6)(x−ni) + (vi/h − hzi/6)(ni+1−x). Stan solves the system with a dense solve. Here it is solved with the Thomas algorithm, which gives the same solution. In the paper the knot values are fitted in Stan using binned Gaia star counts for the sky track and individual stars for distance and velocity.

Natural cubic spline

A curve made from cubic pieces that meet with matching slopes and second derivatives. Here it reconstructs the published stream measurements between knots. Natural means the second derivative is zero at the two ends; the endpoint slopes need not be zero.

Orphan–Chenab stream

A trail of stars spread around the Milky Way, likely left by a disrupted dwarf galaxy. This is the real dataset behind our example: splines summarize its measured track, distance and velocity before a separate physical model is used to study galaxy masses.

Radial velocity

The part of a star’s motion along our line of sight, rather than across the sky. In this walkthrough it is the vertical quantity of the worked spline, measured in km/s. It is one component of motion, not the star’s full speed.

Position along the stream, φ₁

An angular coordinate chosen to run along the stellar trail. Think of it as the x-axis location of a measurement. Moving the selector changes where we evaluate the spline; it does not advance time or move a star along an orbit.

Study: stream coordinates (opens in a new tab)

Galactic standard of rest

Our Sun moves around the Galaxy too. This velocity convention corrects for the component of that solar motion along the viewing direction. The velocity spline and the map’s colour scale use this corrected quantity, often written v_gsr.

Spline knots

The positions where the polynomial pieces meet. Each knot here has a value fitted by the researchers and released with the study. Our curve passes through those values; a knot is not necessarily one observed star. The sandbox uses separate, invented knot values.

Wikipedia: splines and knots (opens in a new tab)

Posterior percentiles

A summary of uncertainty from the researchers’ statistical fit. These two percentiles enclose the middle 68% of the posterior distribution for an individual knot value. The bars belong to the knots; they do not tell us the uncertainty everywhere along the interpolated curve.

Wikipedia: credible intervals (opens in a new tab)

Second derivative

The rate at which a curve’s slope changes. Our solver finds this quantity at every knot, calling it z. Once those values are known, the cubic pieces can be reconstructed. Changing one sandbox knot can therefore affect the bending of neighboring pieces.

Wikipedia: second derivatives (opens in a new tab)

Natural boundary conditions

The extra rules that finish specifying this spline: set the second derivative to zero at both outer knots. These rules select one curve from the possible interpolants. They do not force a horizontal tangent or justify extending the curve beyond the measured range.

SciPy: spline boundary conditions (opens in a new tab)

Tridiagonal system

A matrix whose nonzero entries lie only on the main diagonal and the two beside it. Our smoothness equation connects a knot’s second derivative to its immediate neighbors, which produces exactly this structure and lets us avoid a general dense solve.

Wikipedia: tridiagonal matrices (opens in a new tab)

Thomas algorithm

A specialized elimination method for a tridiagonal system. Here it first sweeps forward to remove lower-diagonal terms, then works backward to recover the knot second derivatives. It solves the same spline equations as the original dense solve, with work proportional to the number of knots.

Wikipedia: the Thomas algorithm (opens in a new tab)

Distance modulus

The difference between apparent and absolute magnitude: how bright a star looks, compared with how bright it would look from 10 parsecs away, on a logarithmic scale. The published distance knots use this quantity. We evaluate its spline first, then convert to kiloparsecs to place the stream in the 3-D map.

Wikipedia: distance modulus (opens in a new tab)

Kiloparsec (kpc)

A distance unit equal to 1,000 parsecs, or about 3,260 light-years. The map uses it because galactic distances are enormous. The distance readout measures from the Sun, whereas the 3-D coordinate system is centered on the Galaxy.

Wikipedia: parsecs and kiloparsecs (opens in a new tab)

Galactocentric coordinates

Coordinates centered on the Milky Way rather than on the Sun. Our 3-D plot uses this frame to place the stream and galaxies together. The gold sightline still starts at the Sun, so its length is a Sun-to-stream distance.

Astropy: the Galactocentric frame (opens in a new tab)

RR Lyrae stars

Pulsating stars whose brightness behavior helps astronomers estimate their true luminosity and therefore their distance. They supply the distance tracers in this study. The shaded gap marks a region with no likely RR Lyrae members; a smooth curve across it is interpolation.

NASA: RR Lyrae distance markers (opens in a new tab)

Tidal stripping

Gravity pulls with different strengths across a small galaxy. Over time, some stars can escape and spread into a trail. That is the physical origin being illustrated here; drawing a spline through today’s measurements does not simulate that stripping history.
